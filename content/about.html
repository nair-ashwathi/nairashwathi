I work at the intersection of stellar astrophysics and gravitational-wave astrophysics. Additionally, I like to think that my work sits at the intersection of theory and observation. My research focuses on understanding how the progenitors of gravitational-wave sources might have formed and evolved.

Currently I focus on the formation and evolution of double neutron stars (DNSs), especially the second detected DNS, GW190425 and blackhole-neutron star binaries (For a detailed overview of my work, see the Research section). On the theoretical side, I model the evolution of massive stars and binaries consisting of a helium star and a compact object using a detailed stellar evolution code called Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA). I use the results from these simulations to further model the supernova (SN) explosion, the kick velocity imparted to the newborn remnant, and the pre- and post-SN orbital dynamics, before integrating these results into a population-synthesis framework.

I am interested in testing the predictions of my simulated models against observations, and in using observations to inform and improve my theoretical models. As the pool of observations grows and we are able to place stronger constraints on our models, this will further strengthen our population-synthesis predictions and our understanding of binary evolution physics as a whole.

Research Interests

I enjoy learning about the formation and evolution of different types of binary systems. Working on my PhD thesis has broadened my interests in massive stars and binary evolution. I am now particularly interested in stripped stars, how their stellar properties change with metallicity, and how this affects their evolution in binaries. I am interested in understanding how the properties of the stripped star influence its companion, the pre-supernova core structure, and the late stages of mass transfer. More recently, my interest has also grown towards understanding the evolution of massive stars at low metallicity.

I completed my Bachelor's and Master's degrees in Mumbai, India, where my interest grew stronger in stellar astrophysics and General Relativity. During my Master's, I became particularly interested in theoretical General Relativity, compact objects, and black-hole interiors. For my Master's thesis, I worked on understanding how the shadows of compact objects observed with the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) are formed. My research focused on the question: *What could the shadow of a compact object be a property of?* I carried out this work at Charotar University of Science and Technology (CHARUSAT), Gujarat, India, under the supervision of Dr. Dipanjan Dey and Prof. Pankaj Joshi.

I then moved to Melbourne, Australia, to pursue my PhD at the Centre for Astrophysics and Supercomputing, Swinburne University of Technology, under the supervision of Dr. Simon Stevenson and Prof. Jarrod Hurley, as part of the ARC Centre of Excellence for Gravitational Wave Discovery (OzGrav).

Moving forward

My current research has left me with many questions, and I would like to continue working on massive stars, stripped stars, and their role in binary evolution.

I am currently looking for a postdoctoral position where I can build on my experience in detailed stellar and binary modelling and population studies. With the growing number of gravitational-wave detections from ground-based and future space-based detectors, I am also excited about incorporating observational data into my future research. I would like to use observations to test and constrain theoretical models, and to better understand the complex physical processes shaping massive stars and binary evolution. Future observational surveys such as BLOeM, Rubin/LSST, and the upcoming Gaia Data Release 4 will provide increasingly detailed observational constraints on massive stars and binary systems, particularly across different metallicity environments. I am interested in combining these observations with detailed stellar and binary modelling to better understand the physics shaping massive-star and binary evolution.
